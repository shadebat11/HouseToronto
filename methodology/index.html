Methodology & sources

How the numbers are calculated

Every threshold on this site can be traced to a stated data source and a stated formula. Here they all are.

Affordability tools are only as trustworthy as the assumptions buried inside them. This page exposes ours in full — the data we use, the formulas the calculators apply, and the things the model deliberately leaves out. If you only read one supporting page on this site, read this one.

1. Where the data comes from

The site combines three published datasets plus a set of standard mortgage rules:

  • Income distributions — Statistics Canada T1FF (Tax-filer) microdata summaries for the Toronto Census Metropolitan Area, fiscal year 2024. These give income thresholds at each percentile, broken out by seven age cohorts (18–24 through 70+).
  • City-wide housing prices — CMHC Housing Market Information Portal figures for the Toronto CMA (2024), used for the all-dwellings, condo, and "other dwellings" benchmark prices and the average condo rent.
  • Regional prices — Toronto Regional Real Estate Board (TRREB) Q2 2025 market data, used for the fourteen-region map (the City of Toronto, the four surrounding regions, and ten of their major municipalities).
  • Mortgage qualification rules — OSFI Guideline B-20 (the stress test) and the federal minimum down-payment rules.
  • Savings-program figures — Canada Revenue Agency rules for the First Home Savings Account (FHSA) and the RRSP Home Buyers' Plan, current as of 2024.

The income figures are best understood as illustrative estimates derived from public T1FF summaries rather than a live feed; they are meant to show the shape of the distribution and the order of magnitude of each threshold, not to be quoted to the dollar.

3. The qualifying-income benchmark (the 22.5% rule)

Most of the affordability checks on the site use a single, transparent shortcut: the gross household income needed to buy a home is roughly 22.5% of the purchase price, per year. So a $651,350 condo implies a qualifying income of about $146,554 (0.225 × 651,350), and a $1,127,744 detached home implies about $253,742.

Where does 22.5% come from? It approximates a typical 30% Gross Debt Service (GDS) ratio under common Toronto conditions — a 20% down payment, a 25-year amortization, and a mortgage rate in the mid-single digits, with property tax and heating costs folded in. Lenders cap the share of your gross income that can go to housing costs (mortgage principal and interest, property tax, and heat) at roughly 30–39%; the 22.5%-of-price benchmark is what that cap works out to once you back-solve for income at representative rates. It is a deliberately simple, stable yardstick so that prices across regions and property types can be compared on a like-for-like basis.

Important: the 22.5% benchmark is a comparison tool, not a pre-approval. The dedicated stress test calculator on the home page models the mortgage payment, property tax, and heat explicitly at the official qualifying rate, and will usually return a somewhat higher required income than the flat 22.5% shortcut. When the two disagree, treat the stress-test figure as the more conservative, lender-like estimate.

4. The mortgage payment formula

Monthly mortgage payments use the standard amortization formula on the financed portion of the price:

Loan principalprice × (1 − down-payment %)
Monthly rate (m)annual rate ÷ 12
Number of payments (n)amortization years × 12
Monthly paymentprincipal × m ÷ (1 − (1 + m)−n)

Unless you change them, the calculators assume a 20% down payment (80% loan-to-value) and a 25-year amortization, with the interest rate set by the global rate slider at the top of the home page (default 5.49%).

5. The mortgage stress test (OSFI B-20)

Federally regulated lenders must qualify borrowers not at their actual contract rate, but at a higher qualifying rate defined by OSFI Guideline B-20:

Qualifying rate = the greater of (your contract rate + 2.00%) or 5.25%.

So at a 5.49% contract rate, the stress-test qualifying rate is 7.49%. The calculator computes the mortgage payment at that qualifying rate, adds estimated property tax and heat, and then divides by the GDS limit to find the income a lender would require. Your actual monthly payment is still based on the lower contract rate — the stress test only governs whether you qualify.

6. Property tax, heat, and the income requirement

To turn a monthly payment into a required income, the stress-test calculator estimates total monthly housing cost and applies a 30% GDS ratio:

  • Property tax — estimated at 0.65% of the home's price per year (divided by 12 for a monthly figure).
  • Heat — a flat $150 per month proxy, the standard placeholder lenders use in GDS calculations.
  • Required income — (qualifying payment + monthly property tax + heat) × 12 ÷ 0.30.

7. A worked example

Here is the full calculation for the calculator's default scenario — an $800,000 home, 20% down, 25-year amortization, 5.49% contract rate:

Home price$800,000
Down payment (20%)$160,000
Loan principal$640,000
Qualifying rate (5.49% + 2%)7.49%
Actual payment @ 5.49%≈ $3,926 / mo
Qualifying payment @ 7.49%≈ $4,725 / mo
Property tax (0.65%/yr ÷ 12)≈ $433 / mo
Heat proxy$150 / mo
Required income (÷ 30% GDS)≈ $212,000 / yr

Notice the gap: the flat 22.5% benchmark would estimate $180,000 for an $800,000 home, while the full stress-test model returns about $212,000. Both appear on the site by design — the benchmark for fast cross-region comparison, the stress test for a lender-like figure.

8. Minimum down payment rules

The down-payment checks follow Canada's tiered federal minimums: 5% on homes priced at $500,000 or less; 5% on the first $500,000 plus 10% on the portion above $500,000 for homes between $500,000 and $1,000,000; and 20% on homes priced at $1,000,000 or more. The calculators flag whether your chosen down payment clears that minimum.

9. FHSA and RRSP Home Buyers' Plan

For the savings-runway and first-home sections, we use the CRA program limits: the First Home Savings Account (FHSA) allows up to $8,000 in contributions per year, to a $40,000 lifetime maximum; the RRSP Home Buyers' Plan (HBP) allows a withdrawal of up to $60,000 per person. A couple combining both can therefore direct up to roughly $200,000 of tax-advantaged savings toward a down payment.

10. What the model does not capture

This is the honest part. To stay transparent and comparable, the calculators leave several real-world costs out:

  • CMHC mortgage insurance premiums on insured (less-than-20%-down) mortgages are not added to the loan.
  • Condo maintenance fees, which can materially change condo affordability, are not modelled in every tool.
  • Home insurance, closing costs, and land transfer taxes (including Toronto's municipal land transfer tax and the first-time-buyer rebates) are not included.
  • Other debts — car loans, student loans, credit-card balances — which lenders fold into the separate Total Debt Service (TDS) ratio, are not considered.
  • Investment growth on your savings, which would shorten the down-payment timeline, is not assumed.
  • Income figures are estimates and reflect a point-in-time snapshot of the distribution, not your verified, lender-assessed income.

In short: the site is excellent for understanding the scale of the affordability gap and comparing scenarios, and it is not a substitute for a lender's assessment or professional advice. See our Terms of Use for the full disclaimer.
